There's a particular kind of bad start to the day: the room is pitch black, everything is silent, and then a phone alarm goes off at full volume. You're awake, but it feels more like being startled than waking up. If you share a bedroom, there's a second version of the problem, where your partner's alarm drags you out of sleep an hour before you need to be up, or yours does the same to them. And if your phone doubles as your alarm clock, it's sitting on the nightstand all night, which makes it very easy to check "just one notification" at midnight.

When the clocks fall back, many people find themselves waking up well before the sun, which tends to make all of this more noticeable. A sunrise alarm clock (often sold as a "wake-up light") is one of the more common ways people try to soften that moment. Instead of going from dark and silent to loud, the light in the room rises gradually before your wake time, and a sound can follow if you want one.

This guide compares three of the best-known options: the Hatch Restore, the Philips SmartSleep Wake-up Light, and the Lumie Bodyclock. One note on scope: this article is about the *waking up* side of these devices: how the light comes up, what the alarm sounds like, how snooze and controls work, whether you need a phone, and how considerate each one can be to a partner. We're not comparing how well they work as sound machines for falling asleep; that's a different job, and we cover it separately.

## What a Sunrise Alarm Clock Actually Does (and Doesn't Do)

### Gradual Light vs. a Sudden Sound

The basic idea is simple. You set a wake time, and some time before it, the lamp starts glowing at a very low level. Over the following stretch it brightens, often shifting from a warm, dim tone toward a brighter one, so that by your wake time the room is lit rather than dark. On most models you can then add an alarm sound that starts at your set time, sometimes beginning quietly and getting louder.

How long that sunrise takes, how bright the lamp gets, and how much control you have over each step varies quite a bit between brands and between models within the same brand. Some models let you adjust the length of the sunrise and the maximum brightness; others are more fixed. Because these details change from model to model and between product updates, we'd suggest checking the current listing for the specific unit you're considering rather than assuming one model in a line behaves like another.

What owners commonly describe is less about any single feature and more about the overall feel. Many people find that waking up in a room that's already getting lighter feels calmer than being jolted awake in the dark by sound alone. That's a comfort and usability point, and it's the main reason these devices are worth considering.

### What We're Not Claiming

It's worth being clear about what this article is and isn't. We're presenting sunrise alarm clocks as alarm clocks: devices that change *how* you're woken up. We're not presenting them as treatment for low mood or any medical condition, and we're not claiming they change your body's internal sleep timing or fix sleep problems. Some manufacturers market their products in broader health terms; we're deliberately leaving those claims out and focusing on the parts you can judge for yourself: the light, the sound, the controls, and how the device fits your bedroom.

If you're dealing with ongoing trouble sleeping, persistent fatigue, or mood changes, that's a conversation for a healthcare provider, not something a bedside lamp is designed to solve.

### Sunrise Alarm vs. Phone Alarm

The most common alternative to a wake-up light is the phone you already own, so it's worth comparing the two directly.

- **The phone stays out of reach.** A dedicated alarm clock means your phone doesn't have to be on the nightstand. For people who find themselves scrolling in bed or checking notifications in the middle of the night, that alone can be the biggest change.
- **Light plus sound vs. sound only.** A phone alarm typically wakes you with sound and vibration. A sunrise alarm adds a gradual light cue before any sound, which some people find makes the sound itself feel less abrupt, or lets them wake before it goes off.
- **Fewer late-night screen checks.** With a phone alarm, setting or checking your alarm usually means unlocking a screen full of notifications. A device with on-device buttons lets you check or change the alarm without that detour.
- **Cost and clutter.** A phone alarm costs nothing extra and takes up no space. A sunrise alarm is another device to buy, place, and plug in.

If your phone already lives across the room and your mornings feel fine, a phone alarm may be all you need. The case for a sunrise alarm is strongest when you dislike waking up in the dark, want your phone out of bed, or share the room with someone on a different schedule.

## Sharing a Bedroom? How to Pick an Alarm That Won't Wake Your Partner

One of the more common reasons people look into sunrise alarms is a mismatched schedule: one person gets up earlier, and the other would rather not wake up with them. A wake-up light can make that easier, but it depends on a few details that are worth checking before you buy.

### Light-Only Wake vs. Sound

The most partner-friendly setup is one where you can wake to light alone, with no sound at all, or with a sound that starts very quietly. Whether a given model supports a light-only alarm, and how low the starting volume can go, isn't something we can confirm across every model, which is why our comparison table lists it as "check current model." Before buying, look for these points in the product description or manual:

- Can the sound be turned off completely, so the alarm is light only?
- Does the sound start quietly and get louder, or does it start at a set volume?
- Is there a backup sound you can set to come on only if you haven't turned the alarm off?
- How easy is the snooze or off button to find in the dark, without waking someone else up?

A light-only setup is less likely to disturb a partner than a loud alarm, but some people wake easily to light, so it may not keep every partner asleep.

### Placement and Light Spill

Where you put the device matters almost as much as which one you buy. A few practical ideas:

- **Keep it on your side.** Placing the alarm on your own nightstand, as close to you as is comfortable, lets the light reach you more than it reaches your partner.
- **Angle it away from them.** If the lamp's light spreads in one direction more than another, point it toward you and away from the other side of the bed.
- **Consider a sleep mask for the partner who sleeps later.** If your partner is sensitive to light, a comfortable sleep mask on their side can help keep the sunrise mostly yours.

None of these setups can promise your partner stays asleep, but together they make it more likely that the person who needs to get up is the one who notices the light.

## Do You Need an App, a Subscription, or Neither?

A lot of the difference between these three comes down to how you want to control your alarm. If you're looking for a phone-free bedside alarm clock, that should probably be your first filter. Here's a quick checklist:

- **"I want my phone out of the bedroom."** Prioritize button-based models, such as the Philips SmartSleep or Lumie Bodyclock lines, and confirm the specific model can be fully set up without an app.
- **"I like adjusting routines from my phone."** An app-based device like the Hatch Restore will likely feel more natural.
- **"I'd use guided content most nights."** It's worth comparing what the Hatch+ membership adds against what you'd actually use.
- **"I only need light and an alarm."** Check whether the base features of whichever device you choose cover that, without any subscription.

There's no universally right answer here. The best fit is the one whose controls you'll actually be happy using half-awake at the start of the day.

## Who a Sunrise Alarm Clock Isn't For

A sunrise alarm is a pleasant upgrade for many people, but it isn't the right tool for everyone. It may not be a good fit if any of these apply to you:

- **You're sensitive to light, or have migraines or an eye condition.** If you're sensitive to light or have migraines or an eye condition, it's worth checking the manufacturer's guidance and talking with your doctor before relying on a light-based alarm.
- **You work rotating shifts or sleep during the day.** If your wake time changes from day to day, or you're sleeping while it's already bright outside, the benefit of a gradually brightening lamp may be hard to notice. For day sleepers, keeping the room dark is usually the bigger challenge. Our guide to [blackout curtains vs. sleep masks for day sleepers]({{ '/posts/blackout-curtains-sleep-masks-for-day-sleepers/' | url }}) covers that side of things.
- **You're a very deep sleeper.** If you already have trouble waking up to a loud alarm, a gradual light and a gentle sound may not be enough on their own. On days when you can't afford to oversleep, keep a backup alarm set as well.
- **Your bedroom is already bright in the morning.** If streetlights or early daylight already fill your room, the difference a sunrise lamp makes may be small.
- **You're dealing with ongoing sleep problems.** If trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking up is a regular issue, a new alarm clock isn't designed to address that. Talking with a healthcare provider is likely to be more useful than any device.
