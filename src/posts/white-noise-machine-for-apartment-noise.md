It's 1 a.m. The neighbors are still talking on the other side of the wall, cars keep passing under the window, and someone upstairs is crossing the floor in hard shoes. If you're looking for a white noise machine for apartment noise, the most useful first question isn't which model to buy. It's which sound is keeping you up.

## What a White Noise Machine Can and Can't Do in an Apartment

### Masking Is Not Soundproofing

A white noise machine doesn't remove any sound from your apartment. The neighbor's voice still comes through the wall at the same level. What the machine adds is a steady sound of its own, which may make that voice less noticeable. This is called masking.

Masking may work less well when the noise is much louder than the machine, much lower in pitch, or sudden. And since a machine adds sound rather than taking it away, your room ends up with more total sound, not less.

### What the Research Says, and What It Doesn't

A 2021 systematic review of noise as a sleep aid rated the quality of the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low. That doesn't prove sound machines never help. It means the research so far can't settle the question.

A small 2026 study published in the journal *Sleep* points in a less flattering direction. It followed 25 healthy adults over seven nights in a sleep lab, using intermittent noise from sources such as aircraft, trains, and cars. Nights with pink noise added on top of that noise had less deep sleep and REM sleep and more time awake than quiet nights, and the added pink noise didn't restore the deep sleep or REM sleep lost to the noise. Foam earplugs, by contrast, were reported to bring back about 72% of the deep sleep lost to the noise.

The study tested pink noise only, not white noise or any consumer machine, and a small group of healthy adults in a lab isn't the same as you in your apartment. We wouldn't read it as proof that sound machines make sleep worse at home. We do read it as a reason to take earplugs seriously, which is why they get a large share of this guide.

### Voices and TV Through the Wall

A steady sound may blur speech enough that the words are easier to ignore. Volume still matters. A quiet conversation is a much easier target than a TV turned up late at night, and bass from a TV or speaker falls closer to the footsteps problem below.

### Traffic and Street Noise

Steady, distant traffic may blend into a machine's sound. Bursts may be harder to cover. A motorcycle, a horn, or a passing truck can jump well above the level of the machine. The 2026 study also used bursts of noise, including cars and trains, though much of it came from aircraft. If traffic is your main problem, earplugs may be the better first thing to try.

### Footsteps and Thumps From Upstairs

We haven't found published research confirming that white, pink, or brown noise covers footsteps. A preliminary 2022 conference paper from Finland, which looked at HVAC noise, suggested that at low frequencies it may not affect how audible walking noise is. A separate paper noted that low footstep sounds may be felt as vibration, not only heard.

Low sounds may be harder to cover than voices, and earplugs can't do much about vibration you feel through the bed or the floor. Neither option is likely to solve this one, so be cautious about any listing that says otherwise.

## White Noise Machine for Loud Neighbors: Machine, Earplugs, or Both?

A white noise machine for loud neighbors and a pair of earplugs take opposite approaches. A machine **masks**: it adds a steady sound so that other sounds stand out less. Earplugs **block**: they reduce how much sound reaches your ear.

- **A machine** leaves your ears free, but it adds sound to the room, may reach the neighbors or a partner, and may do little against sudden loud noise.
- **Earplugs** need a good fit, and some people find them uncomfortable overnight, especially on their side. They also reduce the sounds you want to hear, such as an alarm.

Some people use both. That may be worth trying if earplugs alone leave you noticing every small sound, but we don't have good evidence that the combination works better than either one alone. If you try both, start with earplugs alone, then add the machine at a low volume and see whether anything changes.

## How to Test a Machine in Your Bedroom

One night won't tell you much. Try this checklist:

- [ ] **Start at the lowest volume that helps.** Turn it up only until the noise stands out less, not until you can't hear it at all.
- [ ] **Move it around and compare.** Try it near the wall the noise comes through, then between that wall and your bed, then near the bed but not right beside your head.
- [ ] **Give each sound a few nights.** The noise itself changes from night to night.
- [ ] **Write down what still gets through.** If voices fade but thumps don't, that tells you what to try next.
- [ ] **Check whether your sound is reaching the neighbors.** Stand outside your bedroom door or in the hallway late at night.

## Volume, Alarms, and Safety

**Keep the volume reasonable.** The Sleep Foundation says prolonged exposure to sound above 70 dB may damage hearing. Avoid running a machine at high volume all night. We aren't comparing decibel figures between models because the measurement conditions differ.

**Make sure you can still hear what matters.** The U.S. Fire Administration advises making sure smoke alarms can be heard while you sleep. Before you rely on a machine, earplugs, or both, check that you can hear your smoke alarm, your morning alarm, and anyone in your home who might call for you.

If earplugs or a machine make your alarm hard to hear, a wake-up method that doesn't depend on sound may help. Our [sunrise alarm clock comparison]({{ '/posts/sunrise-alarm-clocks-wake-up-light-comparison/' | url }}) looks at waking with light.

**Handle earplugs with care.** Remove them slowly rather than pulling them out quickly. Throw foam plugs away when they get dirty, and follow the instructions on the package. Stop using them and talk to a doctor if you notice ear pain.

## FAQ

**Can a white noise machine block loud neighbors?**

Not in the sense of removing the sound. A machine adds a steady sound that may make voices or a TV less noticeable, but the noise is still there.

**Will white noise cover footsteps from upstairs?**

Don't count on it. We haven't found published research confirming that it does. Footsteps may be felt as vibration, and low sounds may be harder to cover.

**Is brown noise better for apartment noise?**

It has more low-frequency sound than white noise. We didn't find good evidence that it covers apartment noise better, including footsteps. A machine with several sounds lets you compare.

**Should I use earplugs and a machine together?**

It may be worth trying, but we don't have good evidence that it works better than either one alone. If you try it, confirm you can still hear your smoke alarm and your morning alarm.

**How loud should a sound machine be?**

Use the lowest volume that makes the noise less noticeable. The Sleep Foundation says prolonged exposure above 70 dB may damage hearing, so avoid high volumes overnight, and keep the machine away from your head.
