You get home a little after 8 a.m. and finally lie down. By 10, the garbage truck is working up the street, a delivery van idles outside, and someone next door starts a lawn mower. In the afternoon, the kids get home from school and the doorbell rings. Most advice about a quiet bedroom assumes you sleep at night. If you sleep during the day, that assumption falls apart, and a white noise machine for day sleepers has a different job to do.

## Why Sleeping During the Day Is Harder on the Ears

### Daytime Noise Doesn't Follow a Schedule

Daytime is usually busier and louder than night. Deliveries, construction, yard work, garbage pickup, and people at home all happen while you're trying to sleep, and much of it is hard to predict.

A lot of daytime noise also starts suddenly. A slammed car door or a leaf blower doesn't fade in. A machine's steady sound may blend with steady background noise more easily than with a sharp, sudden one.

### What the Research Says, and What It Doesn't

A 2002 study in *Sleep* by Carter and colleagues looked at noise during the daytime sleep of night-shift workers. In a sleep lab, nine young, healthy women who worked night shifts heard recorded noises, such as trucks and aircraft, during daytime sleep. Their heart rate responded to how loud the sounds were, and their blood pressure responded mainly to sounds that started suddenly. It was a small lab study. It didn't measure sleep quality or health at home, and it didn't look at white noise machines.

A 2021 systematic review of noise as a sleep aid rated the quality of the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low. That doesn't prove machines never help; the research just can't settle it.

A small 2026 study published in *Sleep* points in a less flattering direction. It followed 25 healthy adults over seven nights in a sleep lab, using intermittent noise from sources such as aircraft, trains, and cars. Nights with pink noise added on top of that noise had less deep sleep and REM sleep and more time awake, while foam earplugs largely prevented the loss of deep sleep caused by the noise. It covered pink noise only, not white noise or any consumer machine, and it studied **nighttime** sleep, not sleeping during the day.

There is practical guidance, though. A CDC training module from NIOSH, written for nurses who work night and evening shifts, lists this among its tips for daytime sleep: "Block noises with ear plugs or a machine that creates white noise." That's general advice, not an endorsement of any machine.

Our reading: a machine may be worth trying, but no one can say it will keep your daytime sleep undisturbed. Take earplugs just as seriously.

## What a White Noise Machine for Day Sleepers Needs to Do

Pick the items that fit your shifts. A machine that checks every box isn't automatically the right one.

- [ ] **It turns on and off on its own at set times, or you can reliably stop it by hand.** After a long shift, simple matters.
- [ ] **It can handle more than one time window.** Many day sleepers have a main sleep after work and a nap before the next shift. The NIOSH module suggests napping before returning to work when possible, so a machine that can cover both may be useful.
- [ ] **It's easy to reset when your sleep time moves.** If your schedule shifts on days off or between rotations, look for settings you can change quickly, ideally by day of the week.
- [ ] **It has volume headroom for louder daytime sounds.** That doesn't mean running it at full volume all day.
- [ ] **Its lights won't bother you.** See our guide to a [white noise machine without light]({{ '/posts/white-noise-machine-without-light/' | url }}).
- [ ] **You have a way to wake up in a blacked-out room.** A sound alarm, a light, or both.
- [ ] **You can still hear what matters.** Your smoke alarm, your work alarm, and anyone in your home who might need you.

## Sound Machine for Sleeping During the Day: Setting Up Around Your Shift

**Start the schedule when you get into bed.** The NIOSH module for nurses advises, "Go directly to bed when you arrive home." Set the machine to start around when you usually lie down and stop shortly before you get up.

**Place it between you and the noise.** Put the machine between the bed and the window or door where most of the sound comes in. Don't put it right next to your head.

**Use the lowest volume that works.** Start low and turn it up only until the noise stands out less. The Sleep Foundation says exposure to noise of 70 dB or more can become hazardous over time, so avoid running a machine at high volume for hours. With an adaptive machine, the level you set is the ceiling, so keep that ceiling modest.

**Ask the people at home for help.** The NIOSH module suggests you "Gain cooperation from family and friends so they do not disturb you when sleeping."

**Silence your phone.** The same module says, "Prevent the phone from awakening you."

**Make sure you can still hear what matters.** The U.S. Fire Administration advises making sure smoke alarms can be heard while you sleep. Before you rely on a machine, check that you can hear your smoke alarm, your work alarm, and anyone in the house who might call for you. If you're on call, test this carefully.

**Plan for days off.** If you shift your sleep on days off, set up separate schedules for those days instead of changing settings every time.

## When Earplugs May Be the Better First Step

If your daytime noise is mostly sudden and loud, such as construction or a leaf blower, earplugs may be the better thing to try first. In the 2026 nighttime lab study above, foam earplugs held up better than added pink noise.

Some people use both, though we don't have good evidence that the combination works better than either alone. Earplugs also reduce sounds you need to hear, so confirm you can still hear your alarms.

## Who a White Noise Machine Isn't For

- **People whose main problem is light or an irregular schedule.** A sound machine addresses only a small part of it.
- **People who need to hear their kids, family, or an on-call page.** Masking sound can make those harder to notice. Earplugs can too.
- **People with ear pain, ringing in the ears, or changes in hearing.** Talk to a doctor before adding sound to your sleep.
- **People who can't sleep even on quiet days.** If your sleep problems continue whether or not it's noisy, noise may not be the whole story. Sleep problems related to shift work are worth discussing with a doctor.
- **People who mostly sleep in hotels, dorms, or on the road.** A plug-in bedroom machine may not travel well. See our [portable white noise machine guide]({{ '/posts/portable-white-noise-machine-athletes-hotel/' | url }}).

## FAQ

**Can a white noise machine help me sleep during the day?**

It may make some daytime sounds less noticeable, but the evidence is limited. It may be worth trying, alongside earplugs and a dark room.

**What's the best sound machine for sleeping during the day?**

It depends on the problem: automatic schedules (SNOOZ Pro), noise that rises and falls (Sound+Sleep SE), or waking in a dark room (Hatch Restore 3).

**Should a day sleeper's machine run the whole time I'm asleep?**

Research hasn't settled this. A schedule, a timer, or switching it off by hand can all work; choose what fits your sleep.

**Is white noise or earplugs better for day sleep?**

No study we found answers this for day sleep. For sudden, loud daytime noise, earplugs may be worth trying first (see "When Earplugs May Be the Better First Step" above).

**How loud should it be during the day?**

As low as works for you, and keep an adaptive machine's upper limit modest. See "Setting Up Around Your Shift" above for the Sleep Foundation's guidance on loud sound.
