The snoring starts a few minutes after the lights go out. You reach for a pair of earplugs, roll them in, and it helps, until you turn onto your side. Sleep earbuds look like the obvious upgrade, but they sit in a higher price tier, and nobody wants to spend that much on something that turns out to be uncomfortable on a pillow.

This guide is for side sleepers trying to reduce or mask a partner's snoring. One expectation to set up front: none of these products makes snoring disappear. The realistic goal is to soften the sound enough that it's easier to fall asleep, and easier to drift back off when it wakes you.

## Why Snoring Is Harder to Sleep Through Than Other Noise

### Low, Irregular Sound — What Makes Snoring Hard to Tune Out

Plenty of people sleep fine next to a fan or steady traffic, yet find a partner's snoring hard to ignore. Part of the reason is the shape of the sound. Snoring tends to be fairly low-pitched, and it rarely stays the same. It rises and falls with each breath, pauses, and changes rhythm when your partner shifts position.

Steady sound is easy to file away as background. Sound that keeps changing tends to grab attention again and again. So the useful question isn't only "how quiet can I make it?" but also "how can I make the changes less noticeable?"

### Blocking vs. Masking — The Two Ways Ear Gear Helps

Ear gear works in one of two ways, or a combination of both.

- **Blocking** means physically reducing how much sound reaches your eardrum. Every earplug does this, and so does the fit of a sleep earbud in your ear canal.
- **Masking** means adding a steady, gentle sound, such as white noise, rain, or a fan-like hum, so the snoring stands out less against it. Sleep earbuds can do this because they play sound directly into your ears. Earplugs can't.

Masking is the same idea behind a bedside sound machine, just delivered through your ears instead of a speaker. If you want a fuller explanation of how masking works and how different sounds compare, [our white noise machine comparison]({{ '/posts/white-noise-machines-vs-sound-machines-light-sleepers/' | url }}) covers it in detail.

### Why Side Sleeping Changes Everything

If you sleep on your back, most earplugs sit in your ears without much trouble. On your side, one ear is pressed into the pillow for hours. That pressure can push a plug deeper than you inserted it, shift it out of position, or press the outer part of the plug against your ear in a way that becomes sore by morning.

That's why this guide doesn't rank products by how much sound they block. For side sleepers, the deciding factors tend to be whether the device sticks out past your ear, how soft it is where it meets the pillow, and whether it stays put when you roll over. The NRR (noise reduction rating) on a package is only a rough guide anyway, since real-world results change with fit and sleeping position.

## Earplugs vs. Earbuds: Which Fits Your Situation?

### Start With Earplugs If…

- You've never slept with anything in your ears.
- The snoring isn't every night, or it's moderate rather than loud.
- You don't want another device to charge.

In that case, the lowest-risk first step is a budget pack of <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Macks+Ultra+Soft+foam+earplugs&tag=quietrecover-20" rel="nofollow sponsored" target="_blank">Mack's Ultra Soft foam earplugs</a> or <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Macks+Pillow+Soft+silicone+earplugs&tag=quietrecover-20" rel="nofollow sponsored" target="_blank">Mack's Pillow Soft moldable silicone earplugs</a>. The goal isn't to find your final solution. It's to learn whether you can sleep with something in your ears at all.

The two styles feel quite different. Foam plugs are rolled thin, inserted into the ear canal, and expand to fill it. Moldable silicone is pressed over the opening of the ear rather than pushed inside it, so some people who find foam uncomfortable deep in the ear prefer it. If earplugs turn out to work for you but foam feels sore on your pillow side, Loop Dream is the natural next step.

### Move to Sleep Earbuds If…

- Earplugs help, but the rise and fall of the snoring still cuts through.
- You fall asleep more easily with steady sound in the background.
- You'd like masking sound but don't want a speaker playing it into the shared bedroom, where your partner would hear it too.

If this describes you, the Soundcore Sleep A20 is the lower-risk way to see whether sleep earbuds suit you. If you're already confident in the category, or comfort matters more than budget, consider Ozlo Sleepbuds.

### Can't Hear Your Alarm? Options That Don't Rely on Sound

Worrying about missing the alarm is one of the most common reasons people give up on earplugs. There are a few ways around it that don't depend on hearing anything:

- **A vibrating alarm on your wrist.** Many smartwatches and fitness trackers can wake you with a vibration, which works regardless of what's in your ears.
- **A light-based alarm.** A wake-up light brightens the room gradually before your wake time. See [our sunrise alarm clock comparison]({{ '/posts/sunrise-alarm-clocks-wake-up-light-comparison/' | url }}) if that sounds appealing.

If you use sleep earbuds, many models offer an alarm that plays inside the earbuds themselves, but how it works and how loud it gets varies, so check the listing before you rely on it.

On mornings that really matter, such as an early flight or an important meeting, set a backup alarm or ask your partner to make sure you're up.

## Ear Health Basics When You Wear Something Every Night

A few general habits can help keep nightly use comfortable. These are general suggestions, not medical guidance.

- **Start with clean hands and clean gear.** Follow the manufacturer's instructions for cleaning and replacement. Foam plugs are generally meant to be thrown away after a limited number of uses, while reusable plugs and earbud tips need regular cleaning.
- **Don't push anything in too far.** Insert earplugs only as deep as the instructions describe. When you sleep on your side, the pillow can press them further in, so a slightly lighter insertion may feel better.
- **Pay attention to earwax.** Some people notice that wearing something in their ears every night seems to affect earwax buildup. If that's a concern, ask a healthcare professional rather than trying to clear it yourself.
- **Stop if something doesn't feel right.** If you notice ear pain, itching, discharge, reduced hearing, or discomfort that doesn't go away, stop using the product and talk to a healthcare professional.
- **Keep earbud volume low.** Masking sound only needs to be loud enough to soften the snoring. Stay within the manufacturer's recommended volume range.
- **Remember what you might miss.** Anything that reduces snoring also reduces other sounds, including alarms, smoke detectors, and someone calling for you. Plan for that before you rely on ear gear every night.

## When to Talk to a Doctor About Your Partner's Snoring

If your partner's snoring is very loud, if their breathing seems to stop or pause during sleep, or if they're often very sleepy during the day, encourage them to talk to a doctor. It's worth bringing up gently, since many people don't know how they sound at night.

This guide is about reducing or masking the sound that reaches you. It isn't about the snoring itself, and none of the products here are meant to address it.

## Who These Options Aren't Right For

Consider a different approach if any of these apply:

- **You can't sleep with anything in your ears.** Some people find even the softest plug uncomfortable. If that's you, masking from outside your ears may work better. Consider [a bedside sound machine instead]({{ '/posts/white-noise-machines-vs-sound-machines-light-sleepers/' | url }}).
- **You need to hear what happens during the night.** If you're listening for a child, someone you care for, or a pet, anything that reduces sound needs careful thought. You may want to skip ear gear on those nights or choose an option that only softens sound slightly.
- **You have an ear condition or a history of ear surgery.** Check with a healthcare professional before wearing earplugs or earbuds overnight.
- **You don't want a phone in the bedroom or daily charging (sleep earbuds).** If either of those is a dealbreaker, reusable earplugs are the better fit.

## FAQ

### Can earplugs or sleep earbuds block snoring completely?

It's best not to expect that. Earplugs and sleep earbuds can reduce how loud snoring sounds, and sleep earbuds can add a masking sound on top. But some sound usually still comes through, especially with loud snoring. Think of them as tools that make it easier to fall asleep and stay asleep.

### Are sleep earbuds worth it over regular earplugs?

They can be, for people who find earplugs help but the uneven rhythm of snoring still wakes them, or for people who sleep better with steady sound. If earplugs already work well for you, sleep earbuds may not add much beyond extra charging and upkeep. If you're unsure, start with budget earplugs, and move to sleep earbuds only if you need masking on top of blocking.

### Can I use regular wireless earbuds for sleep instead?

You can try, but everyday wireless earbuds generally aren't designed for lying down. Many stick out of the ear more than sleep-focused designs, which tends to make them uncomfortable on your pillow side and more likely to shift during the night.

### Do these work for travel too?

Yes, with a trade-off. Earplugs are small, light, and don't need charging, which makes them easy to pack. Sleep earbuds can be useful on the road too, but you'll need to keep them charged.
