Red light therapy (also called photobiomodulation) has moved from sports-medicine clinics into home wellness routines over the last few years. Search interest has grown steadily rather than spiking and crashing like a fad, which is part of why it's a good category to write about honestly: the audience is genuinely trying to make a buying decision, not chasing a trend that will be gone in six months.

This guide breaks down the main categories of home devices, what actually matters when comparing them, and who each type is realistically for.

## How red light therapy works, briefly

Red and near-infrared light devices expose skin and tissue to specific wavelengths (typically in the 630–850nm range). Some clinical research suggests potential benefits for skin appearance, muscle recovery, and joint discomfort, though the strength of evidence varies by use case and study quality. If you have a medical condition or are pregnant, talk to a doctor before starting any new therapy — this article is educational, not medical advice.

## The three device categories

### 1. Handheld / small panel devices ($50–150)
Good for spot-treating a specific area — a knee, an elbow, a patch of skin. Sessions take longer since coverage is small. This is the right starting point if you're not sure you'll stick with a routine.

### 2. Mid-size panels ($150–500)
Cover a larger area (torso, back, or full face) in a single session. This is the sweet spot for most home users — enough coverage to treat multiple areas without the price or bulk of a full-body unit.

### 3. Full-body panels ($500–2,000+)
Stand-alone panels tall enough to treat the whole body at once. Makes sense if you're using red light therapy daily as part of a broader recovery routine (post-workout, for example) and want to minimize session time.

## What actually matters when comparing devices

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Wavelength range | Look for devices that specify actual wavelengths (e.g., 660nm and 850nm), not just "red light" |
| Irradiance (mW/cm²) | Determines how long you need to sit at a given distance for an effective dose |
| EMF shielding | Some users are sensitive to electromagnetic fields from panels; better-built units shield for this |
| Third-party testing | Brands that publish independent irradiance test results are generally more trustworthy than those that only cite internal numbers |
| Warranty length | LED panels degrade slowly over years of use — a 2+ year warranty signals the maker expects the product to last |

## Who should start with a handheld device

If this is your first red light device, or you're treating one specific issue (a nagging joint, a small skin area), a handheld unit is the lower-risk way to test whether you'll actually use it consistently before spending on a full panel.
