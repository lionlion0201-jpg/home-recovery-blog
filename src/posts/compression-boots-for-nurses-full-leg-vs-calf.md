The shift is over. You finally sit down on the couch, and your legs feel heavier than the rest of you. You're wondering whether full-leg boots are worth it, or whether something that wraps only the calves would do.

Here's the short version. The real difference between compression boots for nurses and calf sleeves is how much of the leg they cover and how much setup you'll put up with when you're already tired. So start with where your legs feel heavy. If it runs from your feet up into your thighs, full-leg boots match that shape. If it stays in your calves, a calf device covers that area with fewer parts.

## Compression Boots for Nurses: What These Devices Are, and What They Aren't

### A Home Air Massager, Not Prescribed Compression

All three products in this guide are home air massagers. Air chambers inside the sleeves fill and release in sequence, squeezing and then letting go along the leg while you sit or lie down.

They are not the same thing as compression stockings a doctor prescribes, and they aren't a substitute for a pneumatic compression pump a doctor prescribes. They don't replace either one. If you've been told to wear compression stockings, these aren't a reason to stop. And none of them is a way to prevent blood clots.

### What the Maker Says, and What Research Can't Tell You Yet

Hyperice's manuals for the Normatec 3 Legs and Normatec Go describe the intended use the same way: to "temporarily relieve minor muscle aches and/or pains and to temporarily increase circulation to the treated area." Swelling isn't part of that wording. Hyperice's U.S. product pages do list "Reduce tension and swelling" under "Ideal for," but we couldn't find evidence that these home models reduce swelling, so we don't make that claim.

Two small studies of people who stand at work found that leg pain scores and leg measurements went down after a session on a clinical compression pump. The larger of the two followed 39 people who stood for 8 or more hours a day, including teachers, cashiers, and healthcare workers. In that study, pain scores fell more after a 60-minute session on the device than after rest alone. But everyone tried the options in the same order, there was no randomization or blinding, measurements stopped an hour after use, and the device was a five-chamber machine made for clinical use. Most participants also had vein reflux on ultrasound.

The studies were small, short, and used hospital equipment, not the home boots in this guide. So they don't tell us what these models will do for your legs.

Reviews of compression after exercise point the same way: summaries we read describe small effects on soreness and no clear answer on recovery.

## Full-Leg Boots vs. Calf Sleeves: The Shape Decides the Routine

Picture the end of a long shift. Setup and cleanup decide whether you'll actually use it.

### Full-Leg Boots With a Hose and Control Unit

The Normatec 3 Legs is a pair of boots connected by a hose to a separate control unit. You sit down, zip each boot all the way up, connect the hose, and start a session. The manual says to use them sitting, reclining, or lying down, and not to stand or walk in them. Afterward, the boots and unit need a spot to live.

### Wireless Full-Leg Boots

The Therabody JetBoots Prime puts the electronics into the boots themselves. Therabody calls it fully wireless. There's no hose and no separate box, but you still pull on two full-length boots. Therabody's safety page doesn't mention walking in them, so plan on staying seated, as with the Normatec 3.

### Calf Sleeves

The Normatec Go wraps around each calf with a hook-and-loop fastener, and the control unit sits on each wrap. It's the quickest of the three to put on, and the smallest to put away. The trade-off is coverage: it doesn't reach the feet or the thighs.

## Who Should Skip Compression Boots or Ask a Doctor First

The two makers list different conditions, so we've kept them separate.

**What Hyperice's manuals say (Normatec 3 Legs and Normatec Go).** The manuals say to consult a physician before use if you have:

- Deep vein thrombosis (DVT), or episodes of pulmonary embolism
- Acute thrombophlebitis, acute congestive heart failure, or acute pulmonary edema
- An acute infection
- Wounds, lesions, tumors, fractures, or dislocations in the area being treated
- Inflammation, infection, unexplained pain, or bleeding in the area being treated
- Sensitive skin
- A condition where increased venous and lymphatic return is undesirable
- A condition under a physician's care, or one that requires a medical device

The manuals also say people who are unconscious or can't move on their own may be at risk without supervision. The Go's manual adds that it is for adults in good health only.

**What Therabody lists (its air compression devices, including the JetBoots Prime).** Therabody's list of reasons not to use the device includes: known or suspected DVT or pulmonary embolism; a pacemaker, an implanted defibrillator (ICD), or a history of embolism; neurological conditions that cause loss of or altered sensation; acute or severe heart, liver, or kidney disease; decompensated heart failure; swelling caused by congestive heart failure; bleeding disorders; and medications that thin the blood or alter sensation. Therabody lists pregnancy, diabetes, and abnormal sensations such as numbness as reasons to use caution, says to use caution with heart disease, and says high pressure isn't recommended for people with peripheral occlusion disease. Read the full list on its precautions page before buying.

**Our own caution, beyond the manuals.** The Normatec manuals we read don't name pregnancy or diabetes, but Therabody does for its boots. We'd check with a doctor first either way. That includes people who are pregnant, have diabetes with reduced feeling in the feet, have nerve damage, have a heart or circulation condition, take blood thinners, or use a pacemaker or another implanted device. Where Therabody's list says not to use the JetBoots Prime, follow that list.

**When the leg itself needs a doctor, not a device.** Don't use any of these, and see a doctor as soon as possible, if one leg is swollen, painful, warm, or red or discolored. The CDC lists these as possible signs of DVT. If you have trouble breathing, chest pain, or cough up blood, seek medical help immediately. The CDC lists these as possible signs of a pulmonary embolism. If swelling in your feet or legs keeps coming back, is new, or is getting worse, talk to a doctor too. It isn't automatically serious, but it's worth checking.

You spend your shifts noticing these signs in other people. It's easy to put your own down to a long day on your feet. Give your legs the same look you'd give a patient's.

**Simpler options come first.** Resting with your legs up costs nothing. If you've been prescribed compression stockings, keep wearing them as directed.

## FAQ

**Are compression boots the same as compression socks?**

No. These boots and sleeves are home air massagers that squeeze and release in sequence. Compression socks are a garment you wear, and some are prescribed by a doctor. The boots don't replace prescribed socks.

**Can I use compression boots right after a shift while still in my work socks?**

We didn't find a line about socks in the manuals we read, so check your manual and the listing. If you wear prescribed compression stockings, ask your doctor before using an air compression device with them.

**Do leg compression massagers help swelling from standing all day?**

We can't say they do. The intended use in Hyperice's manuals is temporary relief of minor muscle aches and pains and a temporary increase in circulation. Swelling isn't part of that wording. Hyperice's U.S. product pages do list "Reduce tension and swelling" under "Ideal for," but we couldn't find evidence that these home models reduce swelling, so we don't make that claim. The small studies on standing workers used hospital devices, not these models. If swelling keeps coming back, is new, or is getting worse, talk to a doctor.

**Are calf sleeves enough, or do I need full-leg boots?**

Go by where your legs feel heavy. Calves only: a calf device covers that. Feet up to thighs: full-leg boots match that shape.

**Can I walk around in them?**

Not in the Normatec 3 Legs: its manual says not to stand or walk in them. For the Go, Hyperice's product page says it can be used while standing at your desk; this guide assumes you're seated. Therabody's safety page doesn't mention walking in the JetBoots Prime, so stay seated and follow its manual.
